# By Scenario

Four hard conversations, and the ways they differ. The method in `SKILL.md` applies to all four. This file covers what changes.

---

## 1. A commercial change

*A price increase, a term change, the removal of a legacy discount, a move off a grandfathered plan.*

**What makes this one different:** it is the only one of the four where your side is not at fault, and the most common mistake is behaving as though it is. Apologising for a price increase tells the customer your own company made a bad decision, and it invites them to ask you to undo it. You cannot then negotiate.

**The value case comes before the number, and it has to be theirs.** Not your product's improvements over the year. What their business got, measured in the way they measure it. If you cannot state that, the increase is going to be argued on the number alone and you will lose that argument.

**Know what they can benchmark before you name a number.** They will check it against their other renewals, against public pricing, and against what a competitor will quote to displace you. A number that is defensible against those three survives. One that is not gets escalated to procurement.

**Give the notice period properly.** A price change delivered inside their budget cycle is a different conversation from one delivered before it, and the second one is often accepted where the first is fought. If the timing is wrong and you can move it, move it.

**Expect the leverage line.** "At this price we will have to look at alternatives." Decide beforehand whether that is real, and answer it the same way either way: acknowledge it, do not flinch, do not immediately discount. A number that moves the moment it is pushed was never defensible.

**Watch for the request that is actually a concession ask in disguise:** a shorter term, a move to monthly, removal of auto-renew. Those buy them optionality rather than saving money, and they usually cost you more than the discount would have.

**What not to give:** a permanent exception to a structural change. If the increase is a company-wide correction, a private carve-out becomes an audit finding and a precedent your successor inherits. A one-year phase-in is a better instrument than a permanent hold.

Where the account is already in question, read the commercial signals in `renewal-risk` first. A price increase into an unmet economic buyer is a different risk from a price increase into a happy multi-threaded account.

## 2. A roadmap no

*A feature you are not going to build, or not on any timeline that helps them.*

**What makes this one different:** the cost is entirely in the ambiguity, not the answer. A clear no is survivable. The maybe is what does damage, because it keeps the request alive for two years, gets quoted in their internal business case, and turns into a broken promise nobody remembers making.

**Say no in words that cannot be read as a maybe.** "It is not on the roadmap" gets heard as "not yet". "We are not going to build this" is the sentence. If it is genuinely under consideration, say that instead, and attach the date by which they will know.

**Explain it in terms of their problem, not your backlog.** Priorities, resourcing and sequencing are internal facts they did not ask about and cannot act on. What they can act on is whether the underlying job they were trying to do has another route.

**Bring the alternative or admit there is not one.** A workaround, a partner, an integration, a services engagement, or the honest statement that this product does not do this and is not going to. The last one is a legitimate answer and it is occasionally the most respected thing you can say.

**Find out what the request was for.** Feature requests arrive already translated into a solution. The job underneath it is frequently servable another way, and it is also the version worth taking back to product.

**If they built a business case on it**, that is a commercial conversation and not a roadmap one. Find out what was promised, by whom, and in what document, before you say anything else.

**What not to give:** a date you do not control. A hedged commitment on behalf of an engineering team you cannot bind is the fastest way to turn a roadmap no into a missed commitment three months later, at which point you are having conversation 3 instead.

## 3. A missed commitment

*You said something would happen by a date, and it did not.*

**What makes this one different:** it is a trust event wearing the clothes of a schedule event. The customer's real question is not "when now", it is "what else that you have told me is not true". Answering only the first question is why the second one keeps coming back.

**Tell them before the date, not after.** This is the whole conversation. A slip announced three days early is project management. The same slip announced two days late, after they have planned around it, is a credibility problem. The moment you know it is at risk is the moment to say so.

**Name it as a miss.** Not "an updated timeline", not "a revised plan". You said a date and it did not happen. Customers forgive the slip far more readily than the vocabulary used to hide it.

**Re-commit once, later than feels comfortable, and only to a date you control.** The second miss costs several times the first. If the new date depends on someone who has already missed one, do not quote it as a commitment; give the date you will next update them instead.

**Say what changes so it does not repeat.** Briefly. One sentence about the mechanism, not a description of your internal process. And only say it if it is true, because they will check on the next commitment.

**Ask what it cost them.** Most CSMs do not, because the answer might be expensive. Ask anyway: you need to know whether this is an inconvenience or a broken plan with their own executive attached, and those are different conversations from that point forward.

**What not to give:** a credit offered before they have asked, to close the discomfort. It converts a trust conversation into a commercial one and prices your commitments for the rest of the relationship.

## 4. A costly failure

*An outage, a bug, a data problem, a billing error, with a number attached.*

**What makes this one different:** there may be money and there may be liability, so the conversation has constraints the other three do not. It also moves fastest, and the first hour matters more than the rest.

**Facts, timeline, impact, and nothing else, until the cause is known.** No theory. No "it looks like". A speculated cause offered under pressure is quoted back at you when the real cause turns out different, and at that point you are explaining two things.

**Separate what you are doing now from what stops it recurring**, and give them separately. Collapsing them makes the immediate action sound like a permanent fix, and the follow-up then reads as an admission that the first answer was wrong.

**Own the impact before the cause is known.** You can say "this stopped your team working for six hours and that is on us" without knowing why yet. That sentence is available immediately and it is the one they need.

**Do not offer credits, refunds, or anything resembling an admission of liability without commercial authority.** Say plainly that you are asking and give the date. The instinct to make it right in the moment is the correct instinct and the wrong action.

**Ask what it actually cost them**, and get the number if there is one. If there is a number, this stops being a customer success conversation on its own and needs your commercial and legal side involved before anything is written down.

**Update on a fixed cadence, even when there is nothing new.** "No change, next update at four" is a message worth sending. Silence during an incident is read as either chaos or concealment, and both are worse than a dull update.

**The post-incident record is a separate document from the apology**, it comes after the cause is known, and it is written to be forwarded to their risk or security team. Get it read by someone with commercial authority before it goes.

**What not to give:** a root cause on the call, a promise that it cannot happen again, or a number you have not cleared.

---

## The one thing common to all four

Whatever else changes, the sequence does not: say the thing first, take the impact, then explain only if asked, then move to the remedy. Every scenario above gets worse when that order is reversed.
