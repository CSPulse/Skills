# account research

**Find the promise before the customer holds you to it.**

You inherit an account, skim the CRM and the last few notes, and feel briefed. Then in month four the customer mentions, almost in passing, that they bought this to do something the product has never done - a commitment made in an email from last March, by someone who left the company in April, and it's exactly what the renewal will be judged against.

This isn't fit assessment - that question was answered when the deal closed. The job is archaeology: what was sold, by whom, on what promise, and which parts of the story nobody has questioned in two years. It separates what's documented from what's become folklore repeated by people who weren't there, builds a timeline instead of a summary, and ends by naming exactly what still can't be answered rather than guessing.

The failure this exists to prevent: **inheriting an account, reading the CRM, feeling briefed, and discovering in month four that the customer bought it to do something the product has never done.**

Part of the **Read the account** group in [customer-success-skills](../../#readme).

---

## What it actually does

| Step | What you get |
| :--- | :--- |
| Step 1: Find the original promise | The outcome they were told they'd get, in the words used at the time, sourced and dated - or "not documented" stated plainly at the top |
| Step 2: Record vs. folklore | Every inherited claim marked documented, reported, or folklore, so inherited pessimism stops driving how you approach the account |
| Step 3: The timeline | Signature to now, with every contract event, personnel change, and the quiet stretch marked - the period nothing happened, which usually explains the present |
| Step 4: Who is still here | Signer, champion, and sceptic checked against reality, plus anyone new since signature who inherited someone else's decisions |
| Step 5: Usage against the promise | Whether what's actually used matches what was bought, not a generic usage benchmark |
| Step 6: What nobody has questioned | Carried-forward workarounds, stale contract terms, and assumptions nobody has re-examined since onboarding |
| Step 7: The open questions | Split into ask-your-own-side, ask-the-customer, and genuinely unanswerable |

## Who this is for

Anyone taking over an account they didn't originally win or run - a CSM inheriting a transferred book, someone prepping for a first call on an account someone else owned, or re-engaging after months of silence where the account's present has stopped being explained by its file.

It is explicitly not for a prospect you're evaluating before a relationship exists - that's the opposite frame, assessing whether to pursue, and belongs to a sales research skill. This one starts from the fact that they already bought, and asks what they bought and what they were told.

---

## What this needs

**Minimum:** the account name and whatever you were handed - even just a CRM record and a contract produce the structure, the questions, and an honest list of what's unknown.

**Better with:** the original deal notes, mailbox history, support record, and contract. Between them they hold the promise, the sceptic, and the pattern.

**Best with:** twenty minutes with the person who sold or ran the account before you. That conversation holds things no system captures, and the window closes the day they leave.

Missing context never blocks this skill - where the promise can't be found, that absence becomes the headline finding rather than a gap smoothed over, and every unanswerable question gets listed rather than guessed at.

## What this does not do

- It does not benchmark usage. It reads usage against what the account was actually sold to do, which is a different and more honest question.
- It does not treat inherited reputation as fact. Every claim gets marked documented, reported, or folklore before it's allowed to shape how you approach the account.
- It does not simulate knowledge it doesn't have in a first meeting. Where something can't be found, it says so and turns it into a question for the customer instead of a guess.
