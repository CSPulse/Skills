# Context Adjustments

The same observation means different things in different accounts. Read the relevant sections before calling a deviation, and say in the read which adjustments you applied.

---

## By segment

| | Enterprise | Mid-market | SMB |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Nature of risk | Political and procedural | Relational | Mechanical and fast |
| Who decides | Committee, procurement-mediated, on their calendar | One or two people with real budget scrutiny | The user is the buyer |
| Weight most | Stakeholder changes, procurement activity, budget cycle, competing internal initiatives | Single-threading, sponsor engagement, proof of outcome | Usage direction, payment failure, business closure, seasonality |
| Role of usage | Evidence inside someone else's business case | Supporting | Primary, because it is nearly all there is |
| Characteristic trap | Multi-year contracts let risk accumulate silently, then detonate once | Mistaking the champion's enthusiasm for a decision | Trying to read relationship signals that do not exist |
| First read | 9-12 months out, before their planning cycle | Six months out | 30-60 days, and detection has to be automated |

---

## By pricing model

**Seat-based.** Risk is legible but arrives late, usually as a rightsizing demand after the customer has run their own utilisation audit. Watch seat utilisation, headcount change at the customer, deprovisioning activity and licence true-up conversations. Contraction is stepwise and negotiated, so you see it coming - but you see it as a demand.

**Consumption.** There is no renewal event at which to detect risk, and contraction is passive. Track burn-down against commitment pace, projected shortfall, month-on-month volume direction, and any cost-optimisation behaviour. Two inversions matter: falling consumption with stable outcomes can mean the customer got more efficient, which is good; rising consumption can mean bill shock and a smaller commitment next cycle. The real deadline is the date after which they can no longer consume what they committed to.

**Platform or multi-product.** Account-level health averages a growing module against a dying one and tells you nothing. Assess module by module. The leading signal is which module is losing users. Consolidation risk inverts - you are either the consolidator or the consolidated.

---

## By motion

**Product-led.** Behavioural signals are fast and are all you have. Early integration connection, licence utilisation shortly after conversion, and owner-role changes carry most of the signal.

The transition trap: when a product-led account grows large enough for procurement or IT to notice it, its risk profile converts to sales-led overnight. Single sign-on and provisioning requests, security questionnaires, a move from card to invoice, a request for a master agreement - these are expansion signals and risk signals at the same time, and most models read only the first.

**Sales-led.** Usage is a lagging proxy. The relationship map and the business case are primary.

## By industry

Ask what normal looks like here before calling a deviation. These are patterns to check against the account, not rules.

**Financial services and other regulated sectors.** Long procurement, and periodic vendor-risk or security re-attestation. Establish whether a given re-review is routine governance or a re-buy in disguise, because the two look identical from outside. Certification findings can become renewal blockers on their own. A change in the security or third-party-risk lead can matter as much as a champion change. Decisions freeze during audit and close periods. Low usage is frequently the intended state for surveillance and compliance tooling.

**Healthcare.** Clinical adoption is the gate, and decisions are committee-made across clinical, IT, finance and compliance. Integration with the record system is both the switching cost and the failure point - an interface breaking is an account event, not a support ticket. The customer's own IT roadmap can deprioritise you regardless of your performance. Usage by role is decisive: clinician, nurse and back-office activity are entirely different signals arriving as one number.

**Manufacturing and industrial.** Usage is shift-based and seasonal. Breadth across sites matters more than depth per user, so a stalled site rollout is a bigger signal than a usage dip. Watch for plant consolidation, an internal systems programme consuming all IT capacity, and capital-versus-operating budget treatment, which lengthens cycles. Low daily usage is often normal for maintenance-window tools.

**Retail and e-commerce.** Seasonality is extreme and a post-peak collapse is not risk. Renewal timing relative to peak season governs everything, because nobody changes vendors before peak - which pushes the real decision window into the quiet months. A single incident during peak can decide the renewal regardless of the rest of the year. On consumption pricing, volume tracks the customer's own trading, so a falling bill may be their macro problem rather than an adoption problem, and should be named as such.

**Professional services and agencies.** Seat counts track the customer's own utilisation and client wins, so contraction may have nothing to do with you. Project-based usage ends when the engagement ends, which is expected. If the tool is billed through to their client, the risk you are actually carrying is their client's renewal. Margins are thin, so price sensitivity and consolidation pressure are constant, and champion mobility is high.

## A caution on benchmarks

Published churn and retention benchmarks are rarely comparable - monthly against annual, logo against revenue, gross against net, and often with undisclosed methodology. Use them to orient, never to conclude, and do not mix figures from different sources in the same table. The account's own trailing baseline is a better comparator than any industry number.
