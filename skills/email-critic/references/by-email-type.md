# Checks by Email Type

Read the section that matches the draft. Each type fails in its own way.

---

## Meeting recap

Carries six things: what was discussed, what was decided, what the customer raised as a concern or priority, what we owe, what they owe, and what happens next with dates.

Fails when it becomes a "great speaking with you" email with the substance sanded off. The test: could this recap have been written about a different call with the same customer? If yes, it has lost the discussion.

Check specifically:

- Every open item has an owner and, where one exists, a date. "Next week" is not a date.
- The customer's own concerns appear in their framing, not translated into ours.
- Asks are addressed to the named person inline, not gathered into a block at the bottom that everyone has to read to find their bit.
- Nothing was agreed in the email that was not agreed on the call.
- Where the call reversed an earlier plan, the recap says so rather than leaving both versions live.

---

## Follow-up

Answers four things: why now, what is outstanding, why it matters to them, what you need.

Fails as a passive check-in. "Just following up to see if you had a chance to look at this" puts the work of remembering onto the customer and gives them nothing to react to.

Check specifically:

- Is there a real reason to write, or only a reason to nag? New information, a moved date, something done on their behalf, a piece of the work taken off their plate.
- Is the ask small enough to action from the phone? Show what minimal compliance looks like - "names and email addresses are enough" beats "please send the details".
- Has the same ask gone out before? If this is the third chase, the email should change shape, not repeat. Escalate, or remove the blocker for them, or ask what is in the way.

---

## Escalation or bad news

Order matters: acknowledge the impact, own it, explain plainly, say where it stands, say what happens next and by when.

Fails by going defensive, or by explaining internal process the customer did not ask about and cannot use.

Check specifically:

- Does it lead with their impact or our explanation? Theirs comes first.
- Is the ownership real, or hedged into passive voice? "The report was delayed" hides who owns it.
- Is there a date attached to the resolution? Bad news without a date reads as bad news with no end.
- Is it apologising more than once? One clean apology lands. Three read as anxious and shift the customer into reassuring us.

---

## The forwardable note

Written because the customer said "send me something I can pass to legal" or security, or their marketing lead. They will forward it as-is.

Fails by containing anything that is not for the eventual reader. A scheduling ask or an unrelated thread means they cannot forward it without editing, and an email that needs editing does not get forwarded.

Check specifically:

- Would this survive being read by someone who was not on the call and does not know the user?
- Business case before technical detail. The forwarder needs to know why they are passing it on; the specialist needs the substance.
- Does it anticipate the objection? If the customer said most of these requests get declined, write for the person looking for a reason to decline.
- Every claim about the product flagged for verification. This is the email type where a wrong claim does the most damage, because it is read by people whose job is to find it, and the cost lands weeks later.

---

## Executive email

Someone senior, short on time, reading on a phone between meetings.

Fails by burying the point in operational detail they do not need.

Check specifically:

- Point in the first two lines. Everything else is support.
- Outcomes, not activity. What changed, not what we did.
- One ask, and it should be something only they can do. If someone junior could action it, it should not be in their inbox.
- Scannable. If it is a wall of text, they will read the first line and archive it.

---

## Intro request

One paragraph, one ask, no preamble beyond a line of thanks.

Fails by being long. Senior people act on short emails and defer long ones. If it needs a second paragraph, it is not an intro request, it is a briefing with an ask attached, and it should be split.

Check specifically:

- Is the ask the first thing after the greeting?
- Is it obvious what happens after they make the intro, so they know the work ends with them?
- Is the effort minimal? Offering to draft the intro itself often converts a maybe into a yes.
