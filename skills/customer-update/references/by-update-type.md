# By Update Type

Six updates that keep recurring. The method in `SKILL.md` applies to all of them. This file covers what changes.

---

## 1. Incident or outage

**Speed beats completeness.** The first message goes out before the cause is known and says impact, scope, owner and next update time. Nothing else.

**Hold the cadence even with nothing new.** "No change, next update at 16:00" is worth sending. Silence during an incident is read as chaos or concealment.

**Scope precisely.** "Some customers may have experienced" makes everyone check. Say which region, which product area, which time window, so the unaffected can stop reading.

**The post-incident note is a separate document**, sent after the cause is established, written for a risk or security team, and reviewed by someone with commercial authority. Do not merge it into the resolution message.

**Do not offer credits in a broadcast.** It converts an incident into a commercial event across the whole base at once.

---

## 2. A delay to something promised

**Before the date, not after.** A slip announced three days early is project management. Announced two days late, after they planned around it, it is a credibility problem.

**Name it as a slip.** Not "an updated timeline". You gave a date and it moved.

**Re-commit once, later than feels comfortable, and only to a date you control.** The second slip costs several times the first. If the new date depends on a team that has already missed one, give the date you will next update instead of a delivery date.

**Say who is affected in practice.** Most delays matter enormously to a handful of customers and not at all to the rest. That is a segmentation problem, not a wording problem.

---

## 3. A deprecation or end of life

**The longest notice you can give, and expect anger proportional to how long they have had it.** Something in place for five years is in their runbooks, their training and their audit evidence.

**Three things must be in the first screen:** the date it stops, what replaces it, and what the migration costs them in effort and money. An announcement without the migration path reads as abandonment.

**Segment hard.** Heavy users of the deprecated thing need a person and a plan. Everyone else needs one line.

**Expect the "we built on this" reply**, and know before you send what you can offer: an extension, migration help, a grandfathered period. Deciding that during the reply thread is how inconsistent promises get made across a customer base.

**Never deprecate something in the same message as an unrelated announcement.**

---

## 4. A pricing or packaging change

**Contractual notice first.** Check what the agreement requires, in days and in method, before deciding anything else. Getting this wrong turns a commercial conversation into a legal one.

**Named accounts get a conversation before the notice.** A large customer discovering a price change from a mass email is a preventable escalation and usually a preventable negotiation.

**Do not apologise for the decision.** Apologising tells them your own company got it wrong, which is a position you then have to argue out of. Explain what changes and what it means for them. `hard-conversation` covers the spoken version.

**Give the reason once and hold it in the same words.** A justification repeated under pressure reads as an opening position.

**Say what does not change.** On a pricing notice, the unchanged parts are the reassurance, and they are usually left out.

---

## 5. Planned maintenance

**The easiest one, and the one most often written badly.** Subject line carries the date, the window and the duration. Body says what is unavailable, what continues working, and whether any action is needed.

**Timezone explicitly, every time.** A window written in one timezone for a global base generates avoidable tickets.

**Repeat close to the date.** One notice three weeks out is not a notice.

**Tell them when it is over**, in one line. Most vendors do not, so it is unusually cheap credibility.

---

## 6. A change of CSM

**Not an announcement. A relationship event.** Covered in Step 6 of `SKILL.md`, and the summary is: named accounts hear it from a person first, ideally the outgoing one; introduce a human being rather than a role; say concretely what carries over, because that is the actual fear; overlap where you can; and never let them find out from an auto-reply.

**The pattern to avoid:** a generic "your new point of contact is X" to the whole book on the day the previous person leaves. It reads as a downgrade to everyone who receives it, and it is how a stable account starts a renewal conversation asking who they are dealing with now.

**Where the account is mid-escalation or near a renewal, delay the change if you can.** Where you cannot, the incoming CSM does not introduce themselves cold; the outgoing one hands over live.

---

## The one thing common to all six

The reader wants two facts before anything else: **does this affect me, and do I have to do something.** Every type above gets worse when those two are further down than the third line.
