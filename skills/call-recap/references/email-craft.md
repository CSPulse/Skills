# Email Craft

The workspace voice or style guide, if it has one, is the source of truth for how the user writes. Read it. This file covers what that means specifically for post-call emails.

---

## The three rules that matter most here

**Brevity is the point.** A customer email is trying to get a fast response, not tell the whole story. Before writing a line, ask what it is for. Before finishing, ask what can be cut.

**Context belongs in the working document, not the email.** Why an ask matters, what led to it, what happens internally next - all of that goes in the notes under the draft. The customer gets the ask.

**Write as "we".** "We launched", "we are checking with our data partner". The company speaking, not one person reporting. The exception is a personal commitment or an ask only the user can make.

---

## Mechanics

- No em dashes, ever. Single hyphen with spaces, comma, colon or a full stop.
- Paragraphs of one or two sentences. Rarely three.
- Bold the recipient's name in the greeting. Bold other names when addressing several people in one email, so each person can find their bit.
- Bullets for action items and status. Prose for context, relationship and requests.
- Status lists use the state markers: done, in progress, pending. They let someone see the shape of the account in one glance.
- Close forward-looking. Never trail off.

Banned outright: "dive into", "leverage", "circle back", "touch base", "going forward", "as per", "hope this finds you well", "please don't hesitate to reach out", "game-changing", "straightforward".

Phrases that are actually theirs. Build this list from the user's own sent mail rather than inventing it, and reuse the exact wording. A handful of recurring openers, transitions and sign-offs is enough to make a draft sound like them rather than like a template.

## The recap email

Structure that works:

```
Hi **<Coordinator>**,

<One line: warm, specific to this call, not generic.>

<Status marker> **<Thread name>:** <state in one or two sentences,
with the owner and the date where there is one.>

<...one block per open thread...>

Looking forward to <the next concrete thing>.
```

Notes on each part:

- **The opener earns one line.** Specific beats warm-and-generic. "Good to have your data lead join us" says you were paying attention. "It was great connecting" says nothing.
- **Every thread gets a state and an owner.** A recap where three items say "in progress" with no name attached is a status report nobody can act on.
- **Name dates.** "Tuesday, 11 August, 8:00 PM IST" is a commitment. "Next week" is not.
- **Put asks inline, addressed to the person.** Bold their name mid-list. Do not gather all the asks into a block at the bottom, because the person who owns one of them has to read all of them to find theirs.
- **Frame reversals as the customer's idea when they were.** If a senior stakeholder proposed dropping a planned session for a better format, write it as their suggestion. Nobody has to walk anything back and they see their input landed.
- **CC deliberately.** The people who need visibility, not everyone who was invited.

## The forwardable email

When a customer says "send me a note and I'll pass it to legal and security", they are asking for something they can forward without editing. That constrains it:

- Nothing in it that is not for the eventual reader. No scheduling asks, no unrelated threads.
- Business case first, then the detail the specialist reader will interrogate. The forwarder needs to understand why they are being asked; the specialist needs the substance.
- Anticipate the objection. If the customer told you 90% of these requests get declined, that is the brief. Write for the person looking for the reason to decline.
- Offer the artefacts rather than attaching everything. Security documentation, sub-processor lists, DPAs - say they are ready on request.
- End with a specific, small ask. Two named things beat an open-ended one.

Everything asserted in a forwardable email needs flagging for verification. See below.

---

## The intro nudge

One paragraph, one ask, no preamble beyond a line of thanks. Senior people act on short emails and defer long ones. If it needs a second paragraph, it is not a nudge.

---

## The follow-up

A follow-up should answer, in this order: why you are writing now, what is outstanding, why it matters to them, and what you need. "Just checking in to see if you had a chance to review" answers none of those and puts the work of remembering onto the customer.

Where you can, manufacture a real reason to write instead of a bare nudge. New information, a date that has moved, something you did on their behalf, a piece of the work you took off their plate. A follow-up that carries something is read; a follow-up that only asks is deferred.

---

## The internal note

Different rules. Most people write to colleagues in shorthand and it should stay that way.

- No greeting formality, no signature.
- Lead with what they need before the thing they are about to do.
- Include the thing that did not come up but should have. That is usually the most valuable line in an internal note and it never survives into a customer-facing one.
- End with a real question, so it is clear what you want back.

---

## Verification flags

Under any draft asserting something you have not confirmed, say so plainly and name who confirms it. Three categories:

**Product and security claims.** What the software does, collects, stores, costs. These get read by people whose job is to find the flaw. One wrong bullet in a security note kills the whole motion, and the cost lands weeks later when it is unrecoverable.

**Commercial terms.** Pricing, tiers, discounts, contract mechanics. Check them against the signed agreement, not against what was said on a call. Calls compress and people misremember.

**Ownership.** If someone else told the customer they would come back on a topic, an email from the user on that topic cuts across them. Draft it if it is useful, mark who should confirm before it goes.

The flag goes under the draft in the working document, never inside the email body.

---

## Common failures

- **The tidy recap that is the only email.** The recap is the easiest one to write and rarely the one that moves anything. If a call produced one email, check the transcript again for commitments phrased as questions.
- **Topic-shaped emails.** Three emails to the same person, split by subject, when one would do. Group by recipient.
- **The buried ask.** An ask in the fifth paragraph does not get actioned. One ask, near the top.
- **Product enthusiasm in a customer's inbox.** They were on the call. Describe what it does for their named problem, not what it is.
- **Filling a gap silently.** A guessed email address, an invented time slot, an assumed spelling. Ask, or flag.
- **Softening the read.** If the account is at risk, the assessment says so. The user cannot act on a summary that flatters the call.
